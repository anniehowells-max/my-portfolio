Most businesses I speak to don't have an ugly website. They have one that looks perfectly reasonable and still doesn't do its job. People visit, have a look around, and leave without getting in touch or buying anything.

The instinct is often to start again. New look, new platform, new everything. Sometimes that is the right call. But often the problem isn't how the site looks. It's how it works for the people using it. That's what a UX audit is for.

## What a UX audit actually is

A UX audit is a structured review of your website from your customer's point of view. Instead of asking "does this look good?", it asks "can people find what they need, understand what you offer, and take the next step without friction?"

In practice, that means going through your site the way a real visitor would. On a phone and on a laptop. Arriving from Google, from Instagram, from a link a friend sent. Looking at where the journey slows down, where it gets confusing, and where people are likely to give up.

If you have analytics, those numbers are part of it too. Which pages people leave from, how far they scroll, how many start a form and never finish it. The data shows where the problem is. The review explains why.

## Signs your website might need one

You don't need to be in crisis to benefit from an audit. These are the patterns I see most often:

- Plenty of visitors, but very few enquiries or sales.
- People get in touch to ask questions your website already answers.
- Your site has grown page by page over the years and no longer feels like one coherent thing.
- It works well on desktop but feels awkward on a phone.
- You're about to invest in advertising and want to be sure the site can convert the traffic you pay for.

If any of these sound familiar, the issue is usually something specific and fixable, not a reason to throw everything away.

## What you get at the end

A good audit shouldn't leave you with a 40-page report you never open again. It should give you a clear, prioritised list of what to change, with the reasoning behind each point.

Some fixes are quick: a clearer button, a shorter form, a heading that says what you actually do. Others are bigger, like reworking navigation or how a key page is structured. Knowing which is which means you can make the changes that matter most first, whether you do them yourself or bring someone in.

## Audit first, redesign later

Even if you're fairly sure a redesign is coming, an audit is a sensible place to start. It turns vague frustration ("the website isn't working") into a specific brief. You go into a redesign knowing what to keep, what to drop and what to solve, rather than repeating the same problems in a new layout.

And sometimes the audit shows that a redesign isn't needed at all. A handful of focused changes can be enough to make a site that looks fine start working properly too.
